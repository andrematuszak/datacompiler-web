Guides / Chinese document translation

Translate documents to Chinese

Reading time: 5 min

China is one of the world's largest trading partners, and translating documents into Chinese is one of the most common language jobs for European and American businesses: contracts with Chinese suppliers, product certifications, technical manuals, invoices and official paperwork all need a Chinese version sooner or later.

Simplified or Traditional? The first question

Before translating anything into Chinese, you need to know which script your audience reads. Simplified Chinese is the standard in mainland China and Singapore — it is what almost all business documents use today. Traditional Chinese is used in Taiwan, Hong Kong and Macau, and its characters differ visibly from Simplified ones.

DataCompiler produces Simplified Chinese. If your document is headed to Taiwan or Hong Kong, tell us before ordering — translating into the wrong script means the document is unusable for its recipient.

What kinds of documents get translated into Chinese

  • Commercial contracts — supply agreements, distribution deals, NDAs with Chinese partners
  • Certificates and compliance documents — ISO certificates, product conformity, certificates of origin
  • Technical documentation — user manuals, datasheets, installation guides for the Chinese market
  • Trade documents — invoices, packing lists, bills of lading
  • Company documents — business licenses, registration extracts, powers of attorney
  • Academic and professional credentials — diplomas, transcripts, certifications

Why scanned and handwritten documents matter

Here is the part most translation tools get wrong: a huge share of real-world documents are not clean native PDFs. Contracts arrive as scans, photos of signed originals, or pages with handwritten annotations. Generic translation tools simply cannot read them.

DataCompiler transcribes the document in its source language first, then translates. That is why a photographed contract or a handwritten letter produces the same quality result as a born-digital PDF — the OCR step is built into the pipeline, not bolted on.

For more on this workflow, see how to translate a scanned PDF and OCR vs human transcription.

What to expect from the translation itself

Chinese has no spaces between words and no alphabet, so text behaves differently than in European languages: a paragraph that fills half a page in English may take a third of a page in Chinese. The translated text is re-typeset to fit the original document layout rather than dumped as plain text — important for contracts and forms where structure carries meaning.

Names, amounts, dates and legal terms are the highest-risk areas in any Chinese translation. Passages the engine flags as uncertain are routed to a human reviewer before delivery — that review step is where errors like a wrong company name or a shifted decimal get caught.
