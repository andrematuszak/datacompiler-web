Why transcription reduces PDF file size

Reading time: 3 min

A 50-page scanned PDF can easily weigh 50–100 MB, while the same document as editable text weighs a few hundred KB. If you've ever wondered why your scans are so heavy — or how to shrink them — the answer is simpler than most compression tools would have you believe: it's about what's inside the file.

A scanned PDF is a stack of photographs

When you scan a page, you're not storing text — you're storing a high-resolution photograph of the page. Every page becomes a full-color image at 300+ DPI, and images are enormous compared to text. A single letter-size page at 300 DPI is roughly 8–10 MB of raw image data before compression. Multiply by 50 pages and you have a giant file.

A text PDF stores characters, not pixels

A born-digital PDF stores text as font glyphs plus positioning instructions — kilobytes, not megabytes. The same 50 pages of text might total 200 KB. That's a 100–500× size difference for identical readable content.

This is why DataCompiler shows a "smaller" percentage on processed documents: transcription converts the image-stack scan into a rebuilt PDF where the text is real characters again. The visual layout is preserved, but the file collapses in size.

When file size matters

  • Email attachments — most providers cap at 20–25 MB; a 100 MB scan won't send.
  • Cloud storage — archives of scanned documents fill drives fast.
  • Upload limits — many services (including ours, at 100 MB) reject oversized files.
  • Search and indexing — text PDFs are searchable; scans are opaque to search engines and internal search tools.

How to shrink a scanned PDF

Three options, in order of effort:

  1. Re-scan at lower DPI — 150 DPI is often enough for text documents. Quick but lossy.
  2. Compress the images — tools like Ghostscript or online compressors reduce image quality to shrink the file. Works but the document stays unsearchable.
  3. Transcribe it — convert the scan to real text and rebuild the PDF. You get a small file AND searchable, editable, translatable content. This is what DataCompiler does: upload the scan, get the transcript and a rebuilt PDF that shows the size reduction percentage.

The trade-off

Text-based PDFs lose the "look" of the original scan — handwriting, stamps, signatures and exact visual layout don't survive transcription. For archival or legal purposes where the visual original matters, keep the scan. For working documents that need to be searched, edited, translated or emailed, the text version is almost always better.
