OCR vs human transcription: which do you need?

Reading time: 5 min

Every scanned document eventually needs to become text. The question is how: software that reads it automatically (OCR), a person who types it (human transcription), or a hybrid where AI does the first pass and humans fix what it gets wrong. Here is how to choose.

The three approaches compared

OCR software

Fast and cheap — often free — and excellent on clean printed documents: born-digital PDFs turned into scans, typed letters, invoices with standard fonts. Accuracy on good scans can exceed 99%. It collapses on handwriting, poor scans, unusual layouts, tables and anything that is not plain horizontal text.

Human transcription

A person reads the document and types it. Gold-standard accuracy on difficult material — cursive, degraded archives, complex forms — but slow and priced per page or per hour. Turnaround is typically days, not minutes, and cost grows linearly with volume.

AI transcription with human review

The hybrid: a vision-language model produces the draft, and passages below a confidence threshold are routed to a human reviewer. You get machine speed on the easy 90% and human accuracy exactly where it matters. This is the model DataCompiler uses, and it is usually the best cost/accuracy trade-off for business documents.

Decision guide

  • Clean printed scans, large volume, low stakes → plain OCR is enough. Verify a sample before bulk processing.
  • Handwriting, signatures, forms, tables → you need either human transcription or AI with human review.
  • Legal, fiscal or medical documents → never trust unreviewed OCR. Errors in names, amounts and dates are expensive.
  • Tight deadline, moderate volume → AI with human review beats manual transcription on both speed and price.
  • One-off difficult archive document → human transcription may be simplest if you can wait.

What "human review" actually means

Not all review is equal. Weak services silently ship whatever the model produced. A proper pipeline identifies the passages where the model disagrees with itself or falls below a confidence threshold, and routes only those to a reviewer — keeping cost and turnaround close to pure automation while catching the errors that would matter. When evaluating a service, ask how flagged passages are handled and whether you can see the flags in the delivered file.
